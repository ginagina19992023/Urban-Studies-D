# Manufacturing Value on an Empty Island: Platform-Mediated State Entrepreneurialism and the Prosumption of Urban Space on Shanghai's Fuxing Island

*Draft essay — Urban Studies, Aalto University*

---

## Introduction

In December 2024, the Shanghai municipal government unveiled the "Shanghai Quantum City Space-Time Innovation Base" on Fuxing Island (复兴岛), a 1.13-square-kilometre, crescent-shaped island in the Huangpu River that had been held as an undeveloped "strategic blank" (战略留白) for roughly fifteen years (Shanghai Municipal Government, 2024). Eight months later, the island hosted RED LAND, an immersive anime-comics-games (ACGN) event organised in partnership with the social media platform Xiaohongshu, whose single-day tickets sold out in as little as three seconds and which drew nearly fifty exhibitors across an 80,000-square-metre site (Jiemian News, 2025). By May 2026, the island's newly opened waterfront hosted a music festival — attended by an estimated ten thousand visitors over two days — accompanied by thirty thousand redeemable shopping vouchers tying the event directly to over five hundred merchants in a nearby commercial district (Shanghai Municipal Culture and Tourism Bureau, 2026). In eighteen months, an idle industrial island had been converted into a branded, monetised destination.

This essay asks: how is a fifteen-year-old rent gap on an almost-empty industrial island closed through platform-mediated, state-led urban branding, and who captures the value that this process creates? I use Fuxing Island as a diagnostic case — not because its social stakes are dramatic, but because the near-absence of a pre-existing resident population makes an otherwise diffuse process unusually easy to observe in isolation: a state-owned development consortium manufactures a place-brand through symbolic language, landmark objects, and staged events, while visitors' own social-media activity does the work of generating the value that is subsequently converted into trackable commercial revenue. I argue that this constitutes a **platform-mediated form of state entrepreneurialism**, in which the state, rather than a private landlord or platform company, is positioned to capture the value that "prosumption" creates. This central claim about value creation and capture is the essay's core contribution. A secondary and more modest observation — that the reorganisation of the island's daily use around a small number of residual users constitutes a narrow, non-price form of exclusion — is presented separately, with an explicit acknowledgement of how limited the supporting evidence is, since the case's population was already minimal before the branding process began and does not support a strong displacement claim. The essay proceeds by reviewing the relevant literature, tracing the empirical case chronologically, and then situating Fuxing Island against two comparative cases — Baltimore's Harborplace and Helsinki's state-led anti-segregation redevelopment — to identify what is structurally familiar and what is genuinely new about this case.

## Literature Review

### From rent gap to state-led, new-build gentrification

Classical accounts of gentrification begin with Smith's (1979) rent-gap theory: developers profit from redeveloping land once the gap between its current capitalised rent and its potential rent becomes large enough. Hackworth and Smith (2001) periodise this into successive "waves," culminating in a third wave marked by systemic state involvement rather than sporadic public subsidy (Shaw, 2008). Davidson (2018) extends this into an account of "new-build gentrification," in which capital reinvestment, social upgrading, and landscape change occur through entirely new construction on previously non-residential land, frequently under direct state sponsorship, as in China's "state-led" redevelopment model. Fuxing Island fits this pattern closely: a fifteen-year rent gap, opened by deliberate non-investment, is closed through a single, coordinated state-led capital injection rather than piecemeal private reinvestment. The mechanics of this closure are visible in the public record: in July 2025 the listed company Bailian announced an agreement with the Yangpu District Land Reserve Centre to requisition three plots totalling roughly 151,000 square metres — a textbook instance of the state "clearing" land to enable capital reinvestment (Xiao, 2025).

### Legitimising discourse and the question of who captures value

A recurring theme in the literature is that state-led gentrification requires a legitimising discourse, and that the value produced through this process does not accrue evenly. Lees (2008) shows how "social mix" policy is used to reframe gentrification as a corrective to segregation rather than a value-extraction mechanism. Yrjänä (2026), in a Helsinki case study read for this essay, extends this argument and offers two concepts central to this essay's framing: the "reputational gap" (the distance between a place's stigmatised present and its imagined potential, which state actors exploit to justify intervention) and, more importantly, the "subsidy gap" — the initial public investment a state must absorb to de-risk a site before value can be captured, by whoever is positioned to capture it. Fuxing Island's "People's City Island" (人民城市岛) branding — one of three official positionings, alongside "Digital-Intelligence Island" and "Design-and-Art Island" (Xiao, 2025) — performs a structurally similar legitimising function. What Yrjänä's Helsinki case makes clear, and what this essay borrows as its central analytic question, is that the interesting variable is not whether a legitimising discourse exists (it almost always does) but *who is positioned, by the underlying ownership and governance structure, to capture the value that the discourse helps to unlock*.

### Symbolic politics: language, objects, acts

Savini and Dembski (2016), studying the transformation of Amsterdam-Noord from a stigmatised industrial district into a "creative city," argue that political coalitions use three types of symbols — evocative *language* (metaphors such as "the next Silicon Valley"), *objects* (iconic buildings that materialise a narrative, such as an office block built on a preserved crane rail), and *acts* (staged public events that build consensus) — to link a place's industrial past to an imagined creative future. This three-part framework — itself derived from Harvey's (1989) account of entrepreneurial cities competing through the "spatial division of consumption" — offers a precise analytical tool for describing *how* Fuxing Island's brand was assembled, prior to any judgement about its social effects.

### Prosumption as a value-creation mechanism

Existing accounts of Chinese "wanghong" (internet-celebrity) urbanism note that state-led Chinese redevelopment often lacks the classic markers of Western displacement (Cao, forthcoming). More important for this essay's purposes than that absence, however, is what Cao's case study of Guangzhou's Dongshankou district identifies as the underlying *mechanism of value creation*: **prosumption**, whereby visitors simultaneously consume and produce content (check-in photographs shared on Xiaohongshu) that functions as free marketing labour, generating what she terms "digital capital" for the district. This concept — which sits within a longer lineage of thought about unpaid digital labour producing value that is captured elsewhere (a lineage this essay does not have space to fully trace, but which is worth flagging as a next step for a longer version of this argument) — reframes the question this essay asks about Fuxing Island: not primarily "who is excluded" but "who owns the machinery that converts free visitor labour into revenue." Cao also notes, as a secondary finding in her own case, a milder form of exclusion through aesthetic and behavioural distinction rather than price. This essay treats her exclusion finding as a secondary, not primary, analytical lens.

## Case Study: Fuxing Island

### Building the brand: language, objects, acts

Fuxing Island's former identity was industrial: it hosted the Zhonghua Shipyard until production activities relocated off the island in the early 2000s. Rather than being redeveloped immediately, the island was held for approximately fifteen years as a "strategic reserve," a piece of official planning *language* that framed emptiness itself as a form of held value (Xiao, 2025).

This changed in December 2024, when the municipal government unveiled the "Shanghai Quantum City Space-Time Innovation Base" on the island, alongside a dedicated key laboratory and several municipal-level incubation platforms, including the "Fuxing Smart Tower" incubator (Shanghai Municipal Government, 2024). The island was designated a base to "aggregate universities, research institutes, leading enterprises, and industry bodies." This is a clear instance of Savini and Dembski's "object" and "act" categories operating together: the unveiling ceremony is a staged *act* that legitimises the new *language* ("Quantum City," 量子城市), while the roster of prestigious partner institutions functions the way MTV Europe's 2006 arrival did for Amsterdam-Noord — a flagship presence whose association retroactively certifies the district's new identity.

Eight months later, in August 2025, the island hosted RED LAND. Verified reporting confirms that tickets sold out in seconds and that the event drew nearly fifty exhibitors — including IPs such as Pokémon, Aniplex, Honor of Kings, and Genshin Impact — across an 80,000-square-metre site (Jiemian News, 2025). The event preserved the island's industrial heritage as a stage set — most notably the Shipway Park (船台公园), the island's first opened waterfront public space, which retains the physical shipways once used to launch vessels, now repurposed as a public landscape feature (Xiao, 2025). This is precisely the kind of "object" Savini and Dembski describe: a piece of industrial infrastructure re-signified, rather than removed, to lend authenticity to the new consumption-oriented use — echoing Zukin's observation that cultural, rather than economic, value increasingly drives the preservation of old structures.

In May 2026, the island's newly completed 120,000-square-metre north waterfront public space made its public debut with the "Fuxing Island Music Festival," featuring eighteen musical acts on an 80,000-square-metre lawn and drawing an estimated ten thousand visitors, with the four A-grade scenic areas of Yangpu District together receiving nearly 250,000 visitors over the holiday period (Shanghai Municipal Culture and Tourism Bureau, 2026). The event again paired industrial heritage with contemporary popular culture, this time tied to the Chinese animation IP *A Record of a Mortal's Journey to Immortality*.

### The core mechanism: prosumption and the capture of value

What matters most for this essay's argument is not simply that a brand was built, but *how the value generated by that brand was captured*. What distinguishes this sequence from earlier, print- and broadcast-era place-branding (such as Amsterdam-Noord's reliance on newspaper coverage and a national television documentary) is the explicit integration of visitors' own social-media activity into the value-generation process. RED LAND and the subsequent Music Festival were both designed, from the outset, around content generation: attendees' check-in photographs and short videos, shared on Xiaohongshu, function as a form of unpaid promotional labour — precisely the mechanism Cao (forthcoming) identifies as "prosumption." Notably, RED LAND was itself descended from Xiaohongshu's 2023 "Street Life Festival" in Shanghai, which mobilised 2,000 influencers and 400 merchant brands and drew over 200,000 participants (Xiao, 2025) — indicating that this is not an isolated event but the continuation of a platform-driven methodology of urban space production. The Shanghai Culture and Tourism Bureau's own description of the island's strategy, published after the May 2026 festival, frames this candidly as a deliberate "culture-commerce-tourism integration" model, stating that "the core driver of new consumption has shifted from the traditional 'goods plus venue' model to 'experience plus content'" (Shanghai Municipal Culture and Tourism Bureau, 2026) — an unusually frank admission, from the developer's own side, of the mechanism this essay is describing.

Crucially, this visibility was converted into trackable commercial revenue: attendees of the May 2026 festival received redeemable vouchers (30,000 in total, each worth 30 RMB with no minimum purchase) usable at over five hundred merchants in the nearby Wujiaochang commercial district, alongside discounted access to hotels and cultural venues (Shanghai Municipal Culture and Tourism Bureau, 2026). This closes the loop that Cao's Dongshankou case only partially completes: in Dongshankou, prosumption raises a district's platform visibility, which merely *tends to* attract further capital, largely through private landlords raising rents; on Fuxing Island, because the state itself owns the land and organised the platform partnership, the same visitor-generated visibility is captured directly, through a quantifiable, state-designed voucher mechanism, rather than diffusely, through the price mechanism of a private rental market. This is the essay's central empirical finding: **the state, not a private landlord, is positioned as the party that captures the value prosumption creates** — a structural difference from the Western platform-gentrification cases in the literature, where value typically flows to private property owners.

### A secondary observation: residual users and a modest form of exclusion

Fuxing Island's evidentiary record does not support a classic displacement narrative, and this essay does not attempt to build one. Unlike heavily populated, centrally located wanghong districts such as Shanghai's Wukang Road — where an existing residential and commercial community was displaced by an influx of visitors — Fuxing Island had, by design, a very small residential population after fifteen years of strategic non-investment. A primary-source government record from the Dinghai Road Sub-district office documents three "island affairs council" (岛事会) meetings held during RED LAND, convened to negotiate traffic-control arrangements with police, the event organiser, local enterprises, and residents' representatives (Dinghai Road Sub-district, 2025). Delivery workers, water-utility employees, and residents raised concerns about being unable to travel freely; the resolution converted a previously unrestricted right of movement into a permit system, ultimately issuing 1,500 vehicle permits, 900 non-motor-vehicle permits, and 52 special-vehicle permits. Read against Arnstein's (1969) ladder of citizen participation, this consultation *appears*, on the available record, to sit closer to the "tokenism" rungs than to genuine "citizen power": residents' concerns were registered and addressed with permits, but larger questions — whether RED LAND should take place at all — were not part of the consultation's remit. This reading should, however, be treated as provisional rather than settled: it rests on a single, self-reporting government source, with no independent testimony from residents themselves about how much influence they felt they had, and a more generous reading — that the permit system was a responsive solution to a genuine logistical problem during a temporary event — remains equally consistent with the same record. Independent, if similarly modest, corroboration of some degree of exclusion comes from den Hartog's (2021) academic field study of the Yangpu waterfront, conducted years before RED LAND, which found that "the migrant workers who live here seldom use the waterfront spaces, because they have almost no free time." Together, these two pieces of evidence support only a narrow claim: that some residual users experienced a *modest* reorganisation of daily access. They do not support, and this essay does not claim, a stronger narrative of exclusion or harm.

## Discussion

Three comparative cases help clarify what is structurally familiar, and what is genuinely new, about Fuxing Island's transformation — with the comparison organised, in keeping with this essay's central claim, primarily around the question of value capture rather than harm.

**Harvey's (1989) Baltimore** offers the clearest historical precedent for the underlying branding strategy: Harvey shows how Baltimore's 1978 decision to redevelop its inner harbour for private use won the city the title "Renaissance City" — "the triumph of image over substance." The comparison illuminates a structural difference as much as a similarity: Baltimore's harbour redevelopment relied on private developers as the ultimate beneficiaries of the value the image-making unlocked, whereas Fuxing Island's branding process was designed, from the point of land requisition onward, to keep both the land and the value-capture mechanism inside a state-linked structure. Harvey's account of image-substitution therefore describes Fuxing Island's *branding technique* well, but not its *ownership structure* — a distinction that matters more for this essay's argument than the question of comparative social harm.

**Savini and Dembski's (2016) Amsterdam-Noord** shares Fuxing Island's core symbolic mechanics — language, objects, and acts working together to convert an industrial identity into a creative one — but differs in two structurally significant ways. First, Amsterdam-Noord's value uplift was substantially captured by private developers operating under municipal tender, not by the municipality itself; Fuxing Island's value-capture mechanism, by contrast, runs through a state-owned joint venture and a directly administered voucher scheme. Second, Amsterdam-Noord's transformation took over two decades and was repeatedly contested — a populist local party won district elections in 2002 on an explicitly anti-development platform and slowed the process for years — whereas Fuxing Island's equivalent symbolic sequence was compressed into eighteen months with no comparable electoral or grassroots veto point. This suggests that the *mechanism* of symbolic place-branding identified by Savini and Dembski is portable across very different political and ownership structures, but that *who ends up capturing the resulting value* — private developers in Amsterdam, the state itself on Fuxing Island — depends on structural variables (land ownership, political accountability) that vary sharply between the two cases (Feng, Zhang & Wu, 2026).

**Yrjänä's (2026) Helsinki** offers the closest thematic parallel on the question of value capture specifically: a welfare-state government using a benevolent-sounding discourse ("anti-segregation," in Helsinki; "People's City," on Fuxing Island) to open a "reputational gap" and absorb a "subsidy gap" before value can be unlocked. The crucial comparative finding is that even in Helsinki — a liberal-democratic welfare state with a strong planning monopoly and high public land ownership — the state chooses to bridge the subsidy gap primarily *to de-risk investment for private capital*, rather than to capture and redistribute the resulting value uplift itself; Yrjänä's own conclusion is that Helsinki is "well-equipped" to capture value publicly but does not consistently choose to. Fuxing Island, by contrast, demonstrates what public value capture looks like when a state *does* retain the capturing role throughout — via land ownership, platform partnership, and a directly operated commercial mechanism — but without the electoral accountability structures that, in Helsinki, at least in principle keep this choice open to public contestation.

---

## Conclusion

Fuxing Island illustrates a distinctive variant of state-led urban development: a **platform-mediated state entrepreneurialism** in which a state-owned development consortium manufactures a place-brand through the coordinated use of symbolic language, landmark objects, and staged events, and in which the value generated by visitors' own social-media prosumption — ordinarily captured by private landlords or platform companies in Western cases — is instead captured directly by the state, through a concrete, quantifiable mechanism (the voucher-redemption scheme) rather than diffusely through rising rents. This is the essay's central contribution: existing accounts of platform-mediated urban value creation (Cao, forthcoming; Bronsvoort & Uitermark, 2022) largely describe value flowing to private capital; Fuxing Island demonstrates a structurally different possibility, in which the same underlying mechanism — visitor prosumption converting into "digital capital" — is captured by the state because the state, rather than a private landlord, controls both the land and the platform partnership from the outset.

Two implications follow. First, comparative work on gentrification and platform urbanism should treat *who captures the value* prosumption creates as a variable that depends on ownership and governance structure, not as a constant outcome of the underlying mechanism — Fuxing Island and the Western cases in the literature share a mechanism but diverge sharply on this variable. Second, and more cautiously, this essay's secondary observation — that residual users on Fuxing Island experienced a narrow, non-price reorganisation of access, and that the consultation mechanism available to them appears, on limited evidence, to fall short of genuine decision-making power — raises an open question about accountability that this case cannot resolve on its own. Public capture of value is not inherently more equitable than private capture; it depends on whether the capturing institution is answerable to the people whose everyday use of the space it reorganises. Two questions remain open for further research: whether the island's newly built subsidised housing for incoming "talent" is, in practice, accessible to its small population of pre-existing workers and residents, and whether the "island affairs council" model could, with different design, function as a genuine channel for public input rather than a consultative formality. Answering these would extend, rather than currently justify, a stronger claim about who ultimately benefits from a mechanism this essay has shown to be real, well-documented, and — in its ownership structure, if not in its social consequences — genuinely different from its Western counterparts.
