# Housing Development Strategies and Area Reputation in Sydney: Contrasting Waterloo and Green Square

## 1. Introduction: two neighbours, two stories

Two neighbouring precincts in inner-south Sydney, about one kilometre apart, are being rebuilt as high-density neighbourhoods, yet they are told as two different stories. Green Square, a 278-hectare former industrial area, is presented by the City of Sydney as Sydney's "oldest industrial heartland" that "has transformed into a vibrant, sustainable and connected community", with a planned 63,000 residents and about 22,000 jobs (City of Sydney, n.d.). Waterloo's public housing estate, about 2,000 dwellings on 18 hectares, has been called "terrible" and "no longer fit for purpose" by ministers and local members, and long overdue for renewal (Zanardo et al., 2024). Its planning proposal places it "1 kilometre north of Green Square Town Centre" (NSW Land and Housing Corporation [LAHC], 2021, §1.1.2). This essay argues that the two precincts sit inside one planning frame, that of a global city, but are separated by two different stories about their past, and that the planning documents then use the first precinct as a built-form precedent for the second. The distance only locates the estate; the precedent is a separate statement in the planning proposal and its design study (LAHC, 2020, §5.1.7; Turner Studio & Turf, 2020, App. 6, pp. 462–464), set out in section 5. The two descriptions already map onto course concepts: Green Square's is a language of heritage and potential, Waterloo's a language of deficiency and obsolescence, and Zanardo et al. (2024) and Sisson (2024) read the second through the concept of the reputational gap. The cost of transplanting a model onto a place that is already inhabited falls on the people who live there.

The core argument of this essay, that one planning frame hosts two different stories about the past, is built from the four units of the course. From the introductory unit it takes the global city (Sassen, 1991) and the view of urbanisation as a terrain of struggle over land and housing; from the housing unit, the conflict between housing as home and as real estate (Madden & Marcuse, 2016); from the segregation unit, vicious circles and neighbourhood effects (Tammaru et al., 2021); and from the gentrification unit, the rent gap, social mixing and state-led gentrification (Lees, 2008; Yrjänä, 2026). The evidence is policy and planning documents, the consultation record, and three academic studies of Waterloo by Sisson (2024), Zanardo et al. (2024) and Wynne and Rogers (2021). Because these studies already use some of the same concepts, each section below states which concepts they use and what the contrast with Green Square adds. Sisson and Zanardo et al. both describe a "reputational gap" between the estate's present and its idealised future; none of the three studies mentions Green Square in its text. The contribution of this essay is therefore the comparison, and the test of what the idealised future leaves out.

The author's personal note is not evidence, but it explains why reputation matters to the argument that the two precincts are told as two different stories. The author has lived near Redfern, knows the area's reputation well, and still enjoyed walking its streets; the gap between the reputation and the experience is where this essay began. Sections 2 to 5 follow the four course units: the global city, development strategies, reputation, and the promise of mixing and return. Behind the comparison lies a tension between a welfare commitment, the aim of using social housing to break disadvantage, and a global-city ambition, to which the conclusion returns. Throughout, claims about intent are avoided, and the interpretation is marked as interpretation, because documents can show what a policy says and what it uses as a reference, but not what the authors of the policy hoped. In concept terms, a gap between reputation and experience is what the reputational gap names when the reputation is the official one, and what Sennett's pair of building and dwelling names when the experience is the resident's; this application is mine. This keeps the argument about two stories and a transplanted model within what the documents can support.

## 2. The global city: one corridor, two neighbours

The introductory unit supplies the frame that lets the two precincts be read together, which is the first step of the argument that they sit in one planning frame. Sassen (1991) describes a small number of cities, such as New York, London and Tokyo, as hubs that manage and coordinate the global economy, and also as sites of deep inequality and social change; Brenner and Schmid (2021), as presented in the course, treat urbanisation as a global process and a terrain of struggle over land, housing and basic services. Neither concept is applied to Sydney in the lectures, so the application here is an interpretation. For this essay, land and housing are the terrain, and Waterloo and Green Square are two fields of the same struggle.

Sydney's own planning documents state the global-city ambition explicitly, and in them Green Square is named while Waterloo is not, which makes Green Square the precinct whose place in the shared planning frame is documented. The 2014 metropolitan strategy, subtitled "a strong global city, a great place to live", defines a Global Economic Corridor that "extends from Macquarie Park through the Sydney CBD to Port Botany and Sydney Airport" and "generates over 41 per cent of the NSW Gross State Product" (NSW Planning and Environment, 2014, p. 44; the subtitle is as catalogued by the National Library of Australia). The map of the corridor (Figure 15, p. 45) marks Green Square as a strategic centre, the strategy lists it among Sydney's strategic centres, and its Central subregion section commits to facilitating the Green Square Town Centre project, "comprising around 4,000 dwellings and 90,000m² of commercial and retail area" (p. 113). In this document the concept of the global city appears as economic competitiveness: jobs, output and infrastructure. Green Square is therefore an explicit part of the global-city plan.

Waterloo's place in that frame is less direct. The 2014 strategy does not name Waterloo anywhere; it mentions Redfern and the Central to Eveleigh area. On the corridor map, which is at a small scale and does not label Waterloo, the yellow band runs from the CBD south through Green Square towards the airport, and Waterloo lies between the CBD and Green Square, so it falls inside the band by position. This is the present author's reading of the map. Wynne (2018) reads the estate the same way: she writes that Waterloo "sits within this global economic corridor" and alongside the Green Square "strategic centre", citing the same strategy. I did not find the phrase "urban renewal corridor", which she also uses, in the strategy's text, so the corridor claim should be attributed to her interpretation and to the map, not to a statement in the plan. The link between Waterloo and the global-city frame is therefore plausible and geographical, but made by the reader.

Wynne's (2018) ethnography shows what the shared frame means for the residents of Waterloo, who live inside the second of the two stories. She writes that municipalities approach the global-city ambition "by opening up real estate markets for global investment and large-scale development", and that inner-city public housing residents "are aware that they, too, are seen as being in the way of the city's transformation". Sennett (2018) distinguishes the building of cities from the dwelling in them, and defines the city as a settlement in which strangers are likely to meet. In Wynne's account, the government sees the estate's modernist towers "as signifying anachronistic welfare state and city planning ideals", while a resident says of them: "they're good to live in. These buildings were built for eternity". Sennett's pair does not appear in Sisson, in Zanardo et al., or in Wynne and Rogers, so it is one of the concepts this essay adds. Building and dwelling are in tension in Waterloo, and the global-city frame favours the first.

The global-city frame, which the argument treats as shared by both precincts, also meets older claims on the same land. Wynne (2018) reports that members of the local Aboriginal community see the renewal of Waterloo, and the use of land at The Block for student housing, as part of a strategy to remove their community from the area, and she notes that Redfern-Waterloo and nearby Green Square, close to several universities, have become sites of a boom in student housing that is partly aimed at international students. The urban design study for Waterloo South, for its part, says that Waterloo and Redfern are at the centre of the Aboriginal rights struggle in Australia and that understanding the local Aboriginal story is important to the renewal (Turner Studio & Turf, 2020, Part 1, PDF p. 56). In terms of the unit's concepts, this is the terrain of struggle that Brenner and Schmid describe, and it is a reminder that the residents of Waterloo are not one group. For the argument of this essay, the global-city frame places pressure on land that carries more than one history.

Sassen's emphasis on inequality inside the global city gives the shared frame a critical edge that the planning documents lack, and it bears on the first of the two stories. Wynne (2018) draws on Harvey (2008) to describe the global city as one in which "the rich displace the poor through a process of 'accumulation through dispossession'", and she argues that in the discourse of the global city "investment, finance and growth are key, while notions of home, attachment to place and the local are absent". These are strong claims, and the present essay does not adopt them wholesale: the 2014 strategy does not say that it aims to displace anyone, and the Waterloo redevelopment adds social homes. What the critical reading contributes is a question about what the frame measures, since a plan that counts jobs and output will not register attachment to place. That question explains why the same planning frame can contain both a precinct without residents, which it can plan cleanly, and an estate full of residents, which it can renew only by relocating them.

The policy implication for the claim that the two precincts sit in one frame is that the planning frame and the housing strategy are written in different registers. The 2014 strategy measures land by jobs and output, while the social housing strategy of 2016, discussed in Section 4, measures it by the aim of breaking disadvantage; the two documents sit side by side without reconciling these measures. The corridor strategy does not oppose housing: it provides for roughly 4,000 dwellings at Green Square, and in that sense it supports residential growth. But a plan that counts land in terms of the economy has no indicator for the people already living on that land. For Waterloo, this means that the global-city frame strengthens the pressure to treat the estate as land with potential, a point the housing unit develops next.

## 3. Housing: home, land and two development strategies

The housing unit asks whether housing is a home or an asset, and the Waterloo case, which the essay sets against Green Square, shows that the two answers compete. Madden and Marcuse (2016, p. 4) describe "a conflict between housing as lived, social space and housing as an instrument for profitmaking – a conflict between home and real estate", and Aalbers (2016) defines financialisation as the growing role of financial motives, markets and actors in shaping housing. The term financialisation does not appear in the three Waterloo studies, but the idea does, in other words. Sisson (2024) speaks of "neoliberal asset management logics" in public housing policy and of the aim of "value for money"; Zanardo et al. (2024) speak of land values, "highest and best use" and asset recycling; Wynne and Rogers (2021) speak of "land value uplift". The concepts are therefore present under different names, and the question this section asks is how Waterloo and Green Square each treat housing as land, which is the first step in comparing the two stories.

The housing question that Ilmonen frames in the lecture, as it changes from the industrial to the post-industrial city, helps to show how the two precincts carry two different stories. In the industrial city housing is a need, the needs are quantitative and the households are working class; in the post-industrial city housing is consumption, the needs are qualitative and the households are middle class and increasingly diverse. The Waterloo estate belongs to the first period: it was built by the Housing Commission from the late 1940s, and towers such as Matavai and Turanga were designed for people on pensions (Zanardo et al., 2024). Green Square belongs to the second, and the City describes it in the language of choice, as "a great place to live, work and visit" (City of Sydney, n.d.). This reading is my application of the lecture's framework, since neither Zanardo et al. nor the City uses it. It suggests that the two precincts embody two answers to the housing question, and that moving the second model onto the first site replaces housing as need with housing as consumption, in the same place and for different households. This is the sense in which the precedent is a transplant and not only a design.

Waterloo's side of the comparison rests on its strategy, which is stated in the 2016 social housing strategy, Future Directions. Its first action is to "fast-track redevelopment of its social housing portfolio" in partnership with the private sector through Communities Plus, and to "ensure large redevelopments target a 70:30 ratio of private to social housing to enable more integrated communities" (NSW Government, 2016, p. 9). Sites were chosen for land value. Zanardo et al. (2024) report that Communities Plus "major projects" targeted large estates "with high land values, close proximity to employment and economic growth areas, good transport connections", and a history of low-density zoning that allowed intensification; Waterloo "comfortably met" these criteria, and the decision to build a metro station there "further enhanc[ed] land values and ensur[ed] financial feasibility". Wynne and Rogers (2021) likewise link the decision to redevelop to the station and to transit-led "land value uplift". The mechanism is that private development pays for the replacement of social housing, which community housing providers then manage, drawing on exemption from the goods and services tax and on tenants' eligibility for Commonwealth Rent Assistance (Zanardo et al., 2024). In this strategy, the estate's value as land is what makes its renewal affordable to the state, which is the basis on which the Green Square model can be transplanted onto an inhabited site.

The numbers show what the land-based strategy does to the share of social housing, which is the first measure of who bears the cost of the transplanted model. Waterloo South currently has about 750 social dwellings (NSW Government, 2024). The 2026 project plan is for about 3,300 homes, including about 1,050 social, 700 affordable and 1,550 private homes (Homes NSW, 2026). The number of social homes therefore rises by about 300, but they fall from the great majority of the existing homes to about a third of the total. In 2023 the proportion of social and affordable housing for the project was raised from 34 to 50 per cent (NSW Government, 2023). Zanardo et al. add that social housing in Australia is a highly "residualised" tenure, with a small stock and tight eligibility. The concept of residualisation appears in their account, and the figures illustrate it: even where the stock grows, its place in the whole shrinks. The strategy increases the stock while reducing the weight of public housing in the neighbourhood, which is what treating housing as land produces and where the cost of the transplanted model first shows.

Green Square, the precinct used as the precedent, follows a different strategy. It is new construction on former industrial land, led by the City and the state, and the City's page reports that total private development cost will be about A$22 billion, against A$1.8 billion for local public infrastructure, of which the City itself spends about A$710 million including land (City of Sydney, n.d.). White (2014), an author from the City, lists the challenges as flooding, traffic, infrastructure funding and fragmented land ownership, and describes planning mechanisms used "to encourage developers and the state government to partner in the city's vision". The City's page does not mention affordable or social housing. The urban design study for Waterloo South, however, tabulates Green Square Town Centre as having 104 social and affordable dwellings out of 3,308, about 3 per cent (Turner Studio & Turf, 2020, App. 6, p. 463; the figures are read from a scanned table and should be checked against the page). The strategy is the same in logic, since public money prepares land for private housing, but it is applied to land with no inhabitants and results in a very small share of social housing.

A fair account of the transplanted model must also credit what the Waterloo strategy delivers. The 2023 government release says that 70 new social homes at the Waterloo Metro Quarter and over 100 social homes at Elizabeth Street in Redfern will allow some Waterloo South tenants to be relocated in the local area (NSW Government, 2023). At the same time, Zanardo et al. (2024) note that the new social housing is managed by community housing organisations instead of the state housing agency, which shifts both the funding route and the form of management. In Zanardo et al.'s terms the sector is residualised, so even a modest net gain matters to a small stock, which is why the strategy cannot be dismissed as a loss. The point for this essay is not that the strategy fails to add homes, which it does not, but that the way it adds them ties the future of social housing to the value of the land.

The design study also records what the Green Square precedent does carry, and a fair account of the transplanted model should credit it. Among the key lessons drawn from Green Square Town Centre is "Start with the Spaces": the early commitment to providing and delivering community infrastructure is described as a highlight of the Town Centre's planning (Turner Studio & Turf, 2020, App. 6, p. 462). White (2014) likewise describes civic buildings, water recycling and place-making initiatives as part of the City's contribution. If the precedent carries a commitment to public facilities ahead of private housing, it is worth transferring, and the objection of this essay is not to the library or the parks. The objection is to what the precedent leaves behind. In the course's terms, public facilities serve the home side of the conflict between home and real estate; this application of the lecture's concepts is mine. Green Square began as a place without a resident community, so, as far as I could read them, the lessons listed for it concern how to build for newcomers, and not how to rebuild around the people who are already there.

The two strategies can be compared on one measure, the share of social housing, which shows how the second story differs from the first, but the comparison has limits. Waterloo South plans 30 per cent of residential floor area as social housing (LAHC, 2020, §5.1.7), while the Green Square Town Centre is recorded at about 3 per cent social and affordable. The scopes differ: the City's own target in Sustainable Sydney 2030 is that, by 2030, 7.5 per cent of housing in the whole local area will be social and 7.5 per cent affordable rental housing (City of Sydney, n.d.-b), and the 3 per cent refers to one town centre. The difference does not show that Green Square excludes disadvantaged groups by design, for which this essay has no evidence; it shows that the precinct is a market-led one, while the Waterloo precinct is one where the state owns the land and has to reconcile redevelopment with the people on it. The ratio is the measure behind the social mix discussed in the segregation and gentrification units: 30 per cent and 3 per cent are two different mixes. The policy implication is that the same document treats the market-led precinct as a precedent for the state-owned one, a point examined in Section 5.

## 4. Segregation: when the place becomes the problem

The segregation unit supplies the vocabulary in which the story about Waterloo is told, and the policy documents use the same vocabulary in plain terms. Massey and Denton (1988) define segregation as the degree to which groups live separately in different parts of the urban environment. Tammaru et al. (2021, p. 68) explain its reproduction through "the sorting of people into concrete activity sites" and "the contextual effects people experience at these activity sites", and add that the way social housing is distributed, clustered in certain neighbourhoods or spread evenly, shapes this sorting; "advantage breeds advantage and disadvantage breeds disadvantage" (p. 69). The lecture develops this into a chain from spatial clustering of disadvantage, through neighbourhood effects and selective mobility, to wider segregation and immobility across generations (Rosengren, 2026). The NSW documents speak in these terms, so the concepts are present in the policy rather than imposed by the course, and the question of this section is how that language is used to tell the story about Waterloo that the Green Square story is later set against.

Future Directions gives the diagnosis that supports the story told about Waterloo, in these words. About 40 per cent (41,000 dwellings) of social housing in NSW is in concentrated estates, and "while a range of social housing estates function relatively well, many estates experience high levels of crime, unemployment, domestic violence, tenancy management problems, poor educational outcomes and associated child protection issues. These experiences can be passed on through multiple generations, reinforcing the cycle of disadvantage" (NSW Government, 2016, p. 24). The Productivity Commission (2017, p. 205) writes that "a social environment can develop that compounds the effects of disadvantage, and increases the chance that disadvantage is passed on from one generation to the next", citing Vinson (2009). The remedy in the passage on page 24 is Place Plans, which aim at education, training and jobs, and it does not mention demolition. But the same strategy proposes the 70:30 redevelopments "to enable more integrated communities" (p. 9), and a minister's statement on the same page presents Communities Plus as "at the forefront of encouraging people in social housing to be aspirational, not generational". Diagnosis and remedy are in different sections, and the link between them is made in the ministerial language and in the research that follows. This gap between diagnosis and remedy is where the story about Waterloo's reputation takes hold.

Sisson (2024) analyses how that story is built, and his critique has four parts. First, he calls the concept of neighbourhood effects behind "concentrated disadvantage" one that "controversially" locates cause in the estate; second, he notes that tenants are thereby represented, "sympathetically (albeit condescendingly)", as "passive agents whose lives are structured by their noxious environment"; third, he argues that the concentration itself resulted from "targeting, market rents and funding declines", so the focus on the place obscures structural causes; and fourth, he shows how this stigma legitimises renewal, especially where public housing stands on valuable real estate and the state wants "value for money". He draws on Wacquant's concept of territorial stigma and on Kallin's reputational gap, and he describes Communities Plus as articulating reputational gaps "between an estate's supposedly deficient present and its idealized future" in order to pursue rent gaps. Zanardo et al. (2024) extend this: Waterloo suffers several stigmas, in housing and social policy, in planning and design, and in the media, and it is selectively presented as obsolete although there is no evidence that it is among the worst public housing in NSW. These two studies have already made the central argument about Waterloo's reputation, so the contribution of this essay is to set that story against the one told about Green Square.

The phrase "aspirational, not generational" shows how the diagnosis in the story about Waterloo turns into a theory of the tenant. In Future Directions it is a goal for the whole system: "We want to encourage residents in social housing to be aspirational, not generational" (NSW Government, 2016, p. 4); and a minister later joins it to a condition, namely that many new social housing units will be used "to trial innovative programs that link housing assistance to participation in education, training and local employment opportunities" (p. 9). Three readings are possible. It can be read as an honest aim of opening routes out of poverty. It can be read, with Sisson, as locating the cause in the tenant's lack of aspiration, which resembles what Arthurson and Jacobs (2009, as cited in Sisson, 2024) call a social integrationist discourse, in which disadvantage is blamed on isolation and on the conditioning of aspirations by concentration. And it can be read as a step towards conditionality, linking the right to a home to behaviour. The course literature does not analyse this phrase, so the mapping to Sisson's categories is mine. I found no counterpart to it in the official descriptions of Green Square, which speak of place rather than of the aspirations of its residents.

A fair reading of the story told about Waterloo, which the argument about two stories depends on, must also include the counter-evidence. The mechanism behind the diagnosis is not an invention of politicians. The segregation lecture itself treats neighbourhood effects as a real mechanism, with objective effects on education and services and subjective effects on perceptions of the neighbourhood; and the Productivity Commission cites research on children in lower-income neighbourhoods. Sisson's critique is therefore best read as a critique of attribution and remedy, namely that the problem is placed in the estate and the remedy is its redevelopment, not as a denial that concentration of disadvantage can matter. This distinction matters for the essay, because the contrast with Green Square asks where the "problem" is placed, not whether disadvantage is real. A defence of the policy can claim support in the course's own concepts, and the argument here has to answer it. The argument about the transplanted model has to answer that defence.

The lecture on segregation in a retrenching housing regime asks whether the setting of the Waterloo story, a state that funds social housing less directly, is also found in Sydney, and the evidence is partial. Rosengren (2026) describes a drift in Finland from proactive housing policy towards reactive social policy, in which supply-side housing subsidies were replaced by demand-side ones and responsibility for segregation was delegated to local levels. I could not verify that the housing regime in NSW has retrenched in the same way, and the claim is not made here. Two pieces of evidence point in a similar direction. Sisson (2024) writes that the concentration of disadvantage in public housing resulted from targeting, market rents and funding declines. Zanardo et al. (2024) describe a funding route for new social housing that works through community housing providers' tax exemption and tenants' eligibility for federal rent assistance, which is a demand-side instrument. The Finnish parallel is therefore suggestive and not established, and it bears on the argument about the transplanted model because a regime in which the state funds social housing less directly is one in which land value becomes the more important source of funds.

Green Square offers the contrast to the story about Waterloo, and its limits must be stated. The City's description of Green Square is of heritage and promise: "a rich past and an exciting future", an "industrial heartland" turned into a "vibrant, sustainable and connected community" (City of Sydney, n.d.), and White (2014) places the area's problems in flooding, transport, funding and land ownership. I did not find a source that describes Green Square before redevelopment as a stigmatised place, but the search was limited, and an absence in a few sources is not proof of an absence of stigma. A more defensible reading follows from the concept of the reputational gap, which Kallin (2017) introduced and Yrjänä (2026) summarises as holding "the imagined ‘potential’ of a place against the current defamed version", and which Sisson and Zanardo et al. apply to Waterloo: the gap between an imagined potential and a devalued present exists in both places, but in Green Square the devalued thing is land and infrastructure, while in Waterloo it is people and buildings. The difference may follow from the fact that there were no residents on the Green Square site to be stigmatised. This reading is my interpretation. It keeps the contrast between the two stories without claiming that Green Square is stigma-free, and it does not require evidence of exclusion.

White's (2014) account of Green Square's past supplies the first of the two stories in its own words. The area's industrial heritage "dates back to 1815"; by the early 1900s it was "the largest industrial municipality in Australia", with over 500 factories and 22,238 workers; and then, as manufacturing left South Sydney, there followed "this process of decline", a period as an area for waste processing, and a land use that is now "light industrial", generally "up to two storeys high" with a large footprint. White also names the Gadigal people of the Eora nation as the area's traditional owners, so the narrative is not without people, but they stand at the beginning of the story. The account has the same shape as the one told about Waterloo, a deficient present set against a better future, but its subject is a land use and its infrastructure, and not a population: the decline is of factories and waste sites, and the future is a town centre. This fits the reputational gap as Sisson and Zanardo et al. use it, and it shows that the gap can be opened by describing land. The contrast with Waterloo lies in the object of the story, and the object is where the cost of renewal falls.

The policy implication for the argument about the transplanted model is that stigma and redevelopment reinforce each other in Waterloo. The urban design study for Waterloo South states that "the challenge for Waterloo South is to link this community with the established economic centres by increasing links either north towards Redfern or south to Green Square" (Turner Studio & Turf, 2020, Part 1, PDF p. 84). Here the residents are described as a community to be connected, and Green Square is named as one of the destinations. The diagnosis of concentration and the aim of mixing support one another: the first defines the problem and the second defines the remedy. If the problem is the place, the remedy is to change the place, and the land value released in doing so pays for it. This is the pattern which Sisson (2024) criticises, and the Green Square contrast shows its other side: where the land is empty, no such account of the problem is needed.

## 5. Gentrification: mixing, return and the cost of a precedent

The gentrification unit brings the earlier concepts together and asks who bears the cost of the transplanted model. Stein (2019, p. 41), as quoted in the lecture, defines gentrification as the process by which capital is reinvested in neighbourhoods, and poorer residents and their cultural products are displaced and replaced by richer people and their preferred aesthetics and amenities; it is "planned by the state, produced by the developers and consumed by the condo crowd". Smith's (1979) rent gap is the disparity between potential ground rent and the rent capitalised under the present land use. Davidson (2018) extends the concept to new-build development and to indirect displacement; Lees (2008) criticises social mixing as a positive packaging of gentrification; and Yrjänä (2026) shows in Helsinki how anti-segregation policy can serve state-led gentrification, with a reputational gap and a subsidy gap. In the Waterloo literature the label is already in use: Wynne (2018) writes of "the state-led gentrification of their neighbourhood", and Wynne and Rogers (2021) discuss gentrification and cite Lees and Davidson. The contribution of this section is therefore to test what the precedent and the promise mean for the residents.

The rent gap is the part of the account of the two stories that is closest to the documents. Zanardo et al. (2024) show that Waterloo was selected for its land value, that the metro station "further enhanc[ed]" it, and that the estate has a "significant reputational gap, created through denigration of its present use and idealization of its future use", which they connect to ground rents potentially received through partial privatisation and redevelopment. Sisson (2024) shows that public housing is stigmatised in relation to real estate values: because it is public land with low rents, it is said not to be its "highest and best use", and tenants in valuable locations are said to receive "implicit assistance" (Productivity Commission, 2017) or an "in-kind subsidy". The term rent gap itself appears once in Sisson, in the statement that renewal articulates reputational gaps in order to pursue rent gaps, and not at all in Zanardo et al.; the logic is shared, but the vocabulary is not. The rent gap thus explains why the state wants Waterloo's land, and the contrast shows that in Green Square the same gap was closed without anyone to displace.

Davidson's (2018) account of new-build gentrification offers a way to ask whether the two precincts are linked by more than a planning document, and so whether the cost of the transplant spreads beyond the estate. Following Davidson and Lees (2005), as summarised in the course, it identifies four elements of gentrification: the reinvestment of capital, the social upgrading of a locale by higher-income groups, a change in the landscape, and the direct or indirect displacement of low-income groups. Against the view that new building on vacant land is only residentialisation, Davidson argues that indirect displacement can occur when an area becomes harder for poorer people to stay in. Social upgrading and direct displacement cannot be judged for a site that had almost no residents. Indirect displacement would depend on whether neighbouring areas are affected, and I found no study of whether rents or displacement in Waterloo or Alexandria have been shaped by Green Square. The concept therefore leaves an open question that matters for the argument: whether the precinct used as a precedent for Waterloo also acts on Waterloo's surroundings. The documents show a planned link, in the design study's aim of connecting the estate to Green Square, and not an observed effect.

Social mixing is the promise that is supposed to make the transplanted model acceptable to Waterloo's residents, and the evidence on it is divided. Future Directions links the 70:30 target to "more integrated communities", and the Waterloo plans use the language of tenure-blind design, which Sisson and Zanardo et al. both note. The consultation report for the estate found "strong support for social, affordable and private housing to be evenly distributed across the whole of the Waterloo precinct", but "mixed views" on mixing within individual buildings: those in favour felt it "would be more equitable and help support social cohesion", while those against stressed the difficulty of meeting the diverse needs of the different residents in one building (Elton Consulting, 2019). Lees (2008) supplies the sceptical case from the unit: mixing policies are one-directional, they do not in practice produce interaction between old and new residents, and they can erode the support networks that concentration provides. The Elton report shows that residents themselves hold both views. The mixing argument is therefore a legitimate position, and its weakness for the argument about the cost of the transplanted model is that its promise has not been shown to reach the people it names.

Yrjänä's (2026) study of Helsinki, a course reading, offers a parallel that shows what is specific to the Waterloo story. In two stigmatised outer neighbourhoods, Mellunkylä and Meri-Rastila, a new urban renewal model launched in 2020 aimed to deconcentrate disadvantaged and ethnic-minority groups and to change the tenure mix, and Yrjänä argues that the language of anti-segregation, with its statistical measures, gives moral cover to state-led gentrification. She uses the reputational gap, which Sisson and Zanardo et al. also use, and the subsidy gap, in which the state first pays the economic difference in order to de-risk private investment. The structure of the argument, a stigmatised place, a state that owns the land and a policy of mixing, is the same in both cities. The difference lies in the welfare context: Finland's mixing policy draws on a large social housing sector and a long record of social mixing (Rosengren, 2026), while Waterloo's draws on a small, residualised sector, which is why its mixing has to be paid for by private development. The subsidy gap also raises a question about the cost of the transplanted model, namely whether the state's investment in the metro station works as such a first payment; I offer this as an analogy, and I have not found it documented.

For residents, who carry the cost of the transplanted model, the redevelopment is already experienced as a loss of place before any building is demolished. Wynne and Rogers (2021) call this emplaced displacement: displacement that occurs in the absence of physical relocation, and they find that "timeline uncertainty is a key problem" in the experience of residents. Rogers and Wynne (2020) summarise their ethnography of 2010–2017 by saying that "even the threat of being moved has already done significant damage to residents' relationship with their homes and community", at an estate of about 4,000 people in 2018. The consultation found that participants "expressed a strong connection to the Waterloo social housing estate and local area" and stressed that it should remain "an authentic place with its own character" (Elton Consulting, 2019). A 2023 survey of 320 public housing tenants, reported in the press, found that 58 per cent feel part of their community, that 45 per cent would move out if given the opportunity, that 59 per cent feel unsafe walking in the neighbourhood after dark, and that only 33 per cent think their voice can be heard (South Sydney Herald, 2024). These findings show both attachment and dissatisfaction, and they fit the concept of emplaced displacement: the place is valued and the future of the place is uncertain. The cost of the transplanted model is already being carried by those who live there.

Census figures at the suburb level help to place the two areas, but they cannot settle the comparison of the two stories. In 2021 the Waterloo suburb had 16,379 people and a median weekly household income of A$2,028; 72.7 per cent of occupied dwellings were rented and 91.1 per cent were flats. Zetland, which includes Green Square Town Centre, had 12,622 people and a median weekly household income of A$2,192; 70.5 per cent were rented and 92.5 per cent were flats (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2021a, 2021b). Two things follow. Green Square is not an area of owner-occupiers: it is overwhelmingly rented apartments, as Waterloo is, so a contrast based on tenure would mislead. And the near-identical profiles show that suburb data dilute the estate, which had about 4,000 residents in 2018 in a suburb of more than 16,000 (Rogers & Wynne, 2020). In the segregation unit's terms, such data would be used to measure the dissimilarity between areas (Rosengren, 2026), and here they cannot, because both suburbs are dominated by rented apartments. The comparison therefore has to rest on the estate's own sources, such as the consultation and the tenant survey, so that the cost to its residents can be seen.

The imagined future of Waterloo can be traced through five kinds of document, and the precedent is where the comparison with Green Square becomes concrete. The planning documents write the past of the estate in terms of cost and form, saying that its dwellings are "at the end of their economic life" (LAHC, 2020, §2.2) and that it is "an ‘island’, physically disconnected from the surrounding context" (Turner Studio & Turf, 2020, Part 1, PDF p. 72), and they write its future as "a new urban village and local centre" (PDF p. 6). The local member repeats the first story in the language of assets ("many of the buildings have reached their use-by date", NSW Government, 2023), and the Premier, announcing the metro station, said that no one would want tenants "sitting in run down 1950s-style accommodation" (Gerathy, 2015). In 2026 the housing minister, asked why Waterloo should have a mix of tenures, said that "old style estates with thousands and thousands of public homes in the one place" are "not functional communities" (Bowring, 2026), a statement about that kind of estate and not about Waterloo alone. The instruments then tie the future to a model: the planning proposal states that its urban design study "provides a detailed analysis of built form precedents throughout the broader Green Square area" (LAHC, 2020, §5.1.7), and in that study Green Square Town Centre is one of three local "comparative density case studies", alongside Central Park and Crown Square (Turner Studio & Turf, 2020, App. 6, pp. 462–464). Zanardo et al. (2024) describe the effect of such discourse as a reputational gap, created through "denigration of its present use and idealization of its future use"; what the documents add is where the idealised future comes from. On the other side, the residents in Wynne's (2018) study fear that "we'll have something soulless like what's around Danks St", and the paper reports that "global city" renewal projects, "such as at Green Square, are viewed by residents as unattractive places to live". I read the two together as Green Square functioning for Waterloo as an imagined future, but no document says so, and this is an interpretation to be tested rather than a finding.

The promise of return is the point on which the claim about the cost of the transplanted model can be tested, and it cannot yet be tested. The state's commitment is that existing tenants "will be relocated in small stages … and will be given the right to return once the renewal is complete", and that new social homes at the Waterloo Metro Quarter and in Redfern would let some be rehoused locally (NSW Government, 2023). Because the redevelopment is staged over many years, whether tenants return cannot be known now. Advocacy groups argue that the right of return should be granted now and that those relocated report different experiences from those the government publicises (City Hub, n.d.). What can be said is what the concept of emplaced displacement implies: that a right to return does not remove the displacement that occurs while waiting, and that "return" needs defining, to the same place, the same dwelling or the same community. Sisson and Ruming (2024) have developed new measures of the social housing lost and added in estate renewal, which indicates how the promise of return, and so the cost to residents, could be checked once data exist.

The policy question is whether these arrangements strengthen or weaken the gentrification dynamic that the transplanted model brings, and the evidence points both ways. They strengthen it where the funding model makes land value the condition of renewal, where the precedent is a market-led precinct, and where the promise of mixing replaces a measure of who benefits. They weaken it where the number of social homes rises, where the combined social and affordable share was raised to 50 per cent in 2023, and where local rehousing is offered (NSW Government, 2023). A defender can fairly say that more social homes are better than the status quo and that the alternative to value capture has been no renewal at all. The limits of that defence are that the share of social housing falls while the stock rises, that the precedent gives little weight to those who live there, and that the promise of return has not been tested. These policies do not simply benefit or harm the residents; they move the cost of the model onto them in ways that the documents do not count.

## 6. Conclusion and discussion

The comparison of Waterloo and Green Square shows that the issue is not whether one precinct is better than the other, but what happens when a model is transplanted onto a place that is inhabited. Both precincts belong to a global-city plan, as the introductory unit's concepts and the 2014 strategy suggest; their development strategies differ in who owns the land and who pays; their reputations are produced through different stories of the past, which the segregation unit's concepts of neighbourhood effects and the reputational gap help to read; and the planning documents use the market-led precinct as a precedent for the state-owned one. The existing studies of Waterloo, Sisson (2024) and Zanardo et al. (2024), establish that stigma legitimises renewal; the contrast added here shows what the idealised future is made of, and that its social cost is not in the documents' accounts.

A count of the concepts in the existing literature shows where this essay's argument about the two stories adds to it. In the English text of the three Waterloo studies, territorial stigma appears 46 times in Sisson and not at all in the others; the reputational gap appears once in Sisson and twice in Zanardo et al.; the rent gap appears once, in Sisson; words built on "gentrif-" appear 11 times in Wynne and Rogers; and social mixing or tenure-blind design appears in all three. Green Square does not appear in any of the three, and neither does Sennett's pair of building and dwelling. Wynne's (2018) paper uses the global city more than fifty times and mentions Green Square three times, without making the comparison. These counts are a rough guide to emphasis, not to argument, since a concept can be used without the term. They do show that stigma and the reputational gap are already well covered, and that the places where this essay adds to the literature are the Green Square contrast, the building and dwelling distinction, and the test of the promise of return.

The tension between a welfare commitment and a global-city ambition, which sits behind the claim that one planning frame hosts two stories, appears more complicated than a simple opposition. Zanardo et al. (2024) describe social housing in Australia as highly residualised, a small stock with tight eligibility, so Sydney is not a strong welfare city on this evidence, although the extent of its welfare provision more broadly is not something this essay has examined. Within that small sector, the model of Communities Plus uses the land value of estates to pay for the replacement of social housing. On this reading, social housing in Waterloo depends on the same land value that the global-city frame creates, so it is difficult for it to act as a counterweight to that frame. This is an interpretation drawn from documents that I have read, and not a statement found in them. If it is right, the contradiction is built into the funding of the policy, which explains why the promise of mixing and the promise of return carry so much weight in deciding who bears the cost of the transplanted model.

The argument about a transplanted model can be tested, and it is useful to say what would change it. If most relocated tenants returned to the same place within a stated period, the promise of return would be shown to hold, and the cost to residents would be mostly the cost of waiting. If research on Green Square's own residents found that they too feel unheard or unsettled, the contrast between a place told as potential and a place told as a problem would have to be redrawn. Each possibility is open, and none can be settled with the documents read here. They are the reason that the conclusions are stated as interpretations consistent with the evidence, and not as findings about the two stories.

Several planning implications follow from the argument that the cost of a transplanted model falls on residents, and each can be checked against the documents. First, the return promise should be defined and reported: how many tenants return, when, and to the same place or only the same area. Second, the project should publish the share of social housing alongside the number of social homes, since the stock is rising while the share is falling. Third, when a precinct is used as a precedent, the planning documents should say what the precedent does not carry, which here is the difference between land that is empty and land that is a home. Fourth, renewal should consider retention and renovation where feasible; Zanardo et al. (2024) conclude that most of the existing buildings, with the possible exception of the slab blocks, could have their lives extended with relatively modest renovation. Fifth, policy language should avoid locating the problem in the place, which Sisson (2024) criticises, and should give residents a voice. Each recommendation answers a concept: the return promise answers emplaced displacement, the reported share answers residualisation, the explicit precedent answers the distinction between building and dwelling, retention answers new-build gentrification, and the choice of language answers territorial stigma.

The limits of the essay define how far the argument about the two stories can be pressed. I found no evidence on the experience of Green Square's residents or on how Green Square was regarded before redevelopment, so the contrast rests on official descriptions. Suburb-level census figures cannot show the difference between the estate's residents and those of the new apartments: in 2021 Waterloo and Zetland had similar median weekly household incomes (A$2,028 and A$2,192) and similar shares of rented dwellings (72.7 and 70.5 per cent), because the Waterloo suburb includes many private apartments (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2021a, 2021b). I read only parts of the urban design study, and the 2023 tenant survey is known through a press report. Future work should read the whole design study, analyse the estate at a finer geography, and follow the relocation and return of tenants as they happen. Several concepts could not be tested here: indirect displacement, the subsidy gap, and neighbourhood effects at the level of the estate, because the data they need are not in the documents I read. Even so, the main point holds within these limits: when Waterloo is measured against Green Square, the measure leaves out the people who live there.

The essay therefore ends with a question that reframes the original one: what does the Green Square precedent leave out? The usual question is whether Waterloo should become more like Green Square, and the answer depends on what is counted. This essay's answer is the residents, their attachment to the place, and the years in which they wait. Seen in this way, the comparison is not an argument against redevelopment, nor does it argue that Green Square is a failure; it is an argument for including in the measure of a good urban place the people who already live in it. This is the point at which Sennett's distinction between building and dwelling, which no previous study of Waterloo examined, returns: a city is built for its economy, and it is dwelt in by its residents, and a planning model that counts only the first will repeat the pattern each time it is applied.
