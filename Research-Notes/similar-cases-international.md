# Similar cases for the Waterloo vs Green Square essay: estate redevelopment, image reframing, and model/precedent use

Method and honesty note. Every DOI below was resolved in the Crossref API; volume, issue and pages are Crossref values. Abstracts were retrieved via OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar or Crossref. Full text was read only for Soderberg 2024 (open access PDF saved in this folder). All quotes are verbatim from retrieved abstracts unless stated. "Structure" paragraphs are inferred from the abstract unless the entry says full text was read. Nothing here is from memory. Where I could not retrieve an abstract I say so and omit the details.

Important finding on the brief. I found NO study that analyses, as its main object, how a "model neighbourhood" is used as a built-form precedent to justify an estate's renewal. The closest studies cover (a) the construction of estate image and the reframing of stigma, (b) the discourse and documents that justify demolition, and (c) the policy and knowledge base behind it. The precedent mechanism in your essay (planning documents citing Green Square) is therefore your own contribution. You can build on these studies for the stigma and justification half. Do not claim that any of them studies precedent-use.

Terminology flag. "Reputational gap" is not the title of Slater & Anderson. Their 2012 paper is "The reputational ghetto". I verified only that a chapter titled "Opening the reputational gap" exists (Kallin 2017, DOI 10.4324/9781315597607-7). I could not verify from retrieved text that Kallin & Slater 2014 defines the term. Check the term's source before citing it to either paper.

## Case studies

### 1. Mack (2026), Danish "ghetto" demolition, policy documents 1960s to 2018
- Citation: Mack, J. (2026). Liquidating Architecture: Demolition and the Danish 'Ghetto', 1960 to 2018. Journal of Modern European History 24(2), 164-193. DOI 10.1177/16118944261439682. Abstract only (Crossref). The open-access link OpenAlex gave for this paper points to an unrelated scb.se file, so ignore it.
- Structure (from abstract): RQ is how government discourse made demolition the preferred answer to modernist estates. Case is Denmark, culminating in the 2018 "One Denmark without Parallel Societies" document. Data are government documents across six decades. Framework is "liquidation" (afvikling) in three senses. The argument runs in three steps. Estates first stand as welfare-state triumph. Then they become "problems" and sites of capital accumulation. Finally they are marked for demolition through stigmatising descriptions and "dubious statistics". The conclusion is that demolition became "a preferred and celebrated means" of addressing both social conditions and unwanted modernist architecture.
- Evidence channels: planning and policy documents; statistics; discourse. No interviews in the abstract.
- Quote: "relied on stigmatizing descriptions and dubious statistics, including the areas' numbers of so-called 'non-Western' residents to promote this 'liquidation'" and "its construction in words, not walls."
- Relevance (interpretation): this is the closest design to an essay that reads Waterloo's "deficient/obsolete" story from planning documents, with a historical turn from an ideal to a problem.

### 2. Kallin & Slater (2014), Craigmillar, Edinburgh
- Citation: Kallin, H. & Slater, T. (2014). Activating Territorial Stigma: Gentrifying Marginality on Edinburgh's Periphery. Environment and Planning A 46(6), 1351-1368. DOI 10.1068/a45634. Abstract only.
- Structure (from abstract): RQ is how stigma and "regeneration" relate in a stigmatised district. Case is Craigmillar. Data are qualitative research on the Craigmillar Festival Society and the history of the area's stigma. The argument has two steps: the stigma was produced, intensified and contested over time; then the regeneration project's main intention was to attract more affluent residents. Conclusion: stigmatisation and regeneration through gentrification are "two sides of the same conceptual and policy coin".
- Evidence channels: history of the stigma; regeneration project aims; archival or qualitative material on residents' organisation (as the abstract states).
- Quote: "the 'blemish of place' becomes a target and rationale for 'fixing' the area, thus obviating and obstructing policies aimed at attacking deprivation". Also: "The state's role in creating the very stigma it then insists on scrubbing highlights a major contradiction in contemporary urban policy."
- Relevance (interpretation): the state-produces-then-cures-stigma argument fits Waterloo's "no longer fit for purpose" story and its link to the course's state-led gentrification.

### 3. Arthurson (2001), six Australian estates, "physical image construction"
- Citation: Arthurson, K. (2001). Achieving Social Justice in Estate Regeneration: The Impact of Physical Image Construction. Housing Studies 16(6), 807-826. DOI 10.1080/02673030120090557. Abstract only.
- Structure (from abstract): RQ is built on three explicit questions: in whose images estates are recreated (authorities, developers or residents); whether images are imposed or democratised; and the policy consequences. Case is six Australian estates under regeneration. Data and sections are not stated in the abstract. Conclusion: buying and selling of place through image reconstruction "to attract more affluent residents" likely adds to rather than reduces inequality for existing tenants.
- Evidence channels: not stated in the abstract (physical design and image).
- Quote: "In whose images the estates are being recreated, housing authorities, private property developers or residents?"
- Relevance (interpretation): this is an Australian precedent for treating the built form and the image of a rebuilt estate as the object, which fits your Green Square as image source.

### 4. Lees (2014; online 2013), Aylesbury Estate, London
- Citation: Lees, L. (2014). The Urban Injustices of New Labour's "New Urban Renewal": The Case of the Aylesbury Estate in London. Antipode 46(4), 921-947. DOI 10.1111/anti.12020. Crossref gives issued year 2013 (online); volume and pages are for the print issue. Abstract only. Lees 2008 is already in your essay, but this is a different paper.
- Structure (from abstract): case of state-led gentrification via mixed communities policy. Argument steps: tenant support was "manipulated and misrepresented"; choices were closed down to a "false choice" between unwanted regeneration and decline; residents' views and resistance. Concludes that dissent persists and calls for re-establishing the city as driver of democratic politics.
- Evidence channels: consultation and ballot process; resident interviews; policy (the abstract mentions tenant support and resistance). Planning documents are not stated.
- Quote: "state-led gentrification of British council estates, undertaken through the guise of mixed communities policy".
- Relevance (interpretation): the "false choice" between renewal and decline parallels the consultation record you use for Waterloo.

### 5. Lees & Ferreri (2016), Heygate Estate / Elephant and Castle
- Citation: Lees, L. & Ferreri, M. (2016). Resisting gentrification on its final frontiers: Learning from the Heygate Estate in London (1974-2013). Cities 57, 14-24. DOI 10.1016/j.cities.2015.12.005. Abstract only (open-access repository version exists at dro.dur.ac.uk/26087, which blocked my download with a bot check).
- Structure (from abstract): scholar-activist project. Three forms of resistance (planning engagement, keeping the estate open during displacement, legal challenge to the Compulsory Purchase Order). Conclusion: although the estate was demolished, resistance exposed that the regeneration "was/is not in the 'public interest'" and discredited "regeneration spin".
- Evidence channels: planning process; CPO public inquiry; activism.
- Quote: "discrediting the local authority's 'regeneration spin'."
- Relevance (interpretation): useful if the essay wants a section on how official stories were contested.

### 6. Kipfer & Petrunia (2009), Regent Park, Toronto
- Citation: Kipfer, S. & Petrunia, J. (2009). "Recolonization" and public housing: a Toronto case study. Studies in Political Economy 83(1), 111-139. DOI 10.1080/19187033.2009.11675058. Abstract only (OpenAlex).
- Structure (from abstract): critique of "Third Way urbanism" and "place-based" policy limits, using Lefebvre and Fanon. Case Regent Park. Argument: the redevelopment is a three-pronged economic, social and cultural strategy.
- Quote: "the redevelopment project of Regent Park is best understood as a three-pronged, profoundly racialized economic, social, and cultural strategy to recolonize a long-pathologized and segregated, but potentially valuable central city social space in the name of 'diversity' and 'social mixity.'"
- Relevance (interpretation): the "pathologized but valuable" central site matches the rent-gap reading of Waterloo's location.
- Related, existence verified, abstract NOT retrieved: August, M. (2016). "It's all about power and you have none": The marginalization of tenant resistance to mixed-income social housing redevelopment in Toronto, Canada. Cities 57, 25-32. DOI 10.1016/j.cities.2015.12.004. NOT VERIFIED beyond bibliographic data.

### 7. Uitermark, Duyvendak & Kleinhans (2007), Hoogvliet, Rotterdam
- Citation: Uitermark, J., Duyvendak, J. W. & Kleinhans, R. (2007). Gentrification as a Governmental Strategy: Social Control and Social Cohesion in Hoogvliet, Rotterdam. Environment and Planning A 39(1), 125-141. DOI 10.1068/a39142. Abstract only.
- Structure (from abstract): RQ is what drives state-led gentrification in Dutch peripheral estates. Argument: not tax base, developer profit or middle-class demand but state actors and housing associations seeking social order. Then residents' support, then superficial or hostile mixing, then the conclusion that gentrification undermines cohesion.
- Quote: "Gentrification is used to pacify tensions and to reduce concentrations that pose a problem for authorities."
- Relevance (interpretation): offers a contrasting explanation (social order versus rent gap) to test against Waterloo.

### 8. Arthurson, Levin & Ziersch (2015), Carlton, Melbourne
- Citation: Arthurson, K., Levin, I. & Ziersch, A. (2015). What is the Meaning of 'Social Mix'? Shifting perspectives in planning and implementing public housing estate redevelopment. Australian Geographer 46(4), 491-505. DOI 10.1080/00049182.2015.1075270. This is already in your bibliography, so not new. Included only because it is the Australian comparator and its abstract states the design: interviews with tenants, homeowners, homebuyers, private renters and service providers; social mix as inclusion versus state-led gentrification; finding that after the GFC mix was "perceived as a means to harness market capital and attract higher income residents". The companion paper Levin, Arthurson & Ziersch (2014), Cities 40, 23-31, DOI 10.1016/j.cities.2014.04.002, on design at Carlton, exists but I could not retrieve its abstract. NOT VERIFIED beyond bibliographic data.

### 9. Soderberg (2024), Mjolnerparken, Copenhagen (full text read)
- Citation: Soderberg, R. (2024). 'This is not a ghetto': Residents' resistance and re-negotiation of neighbourhood narratives. Radical Housing Journal 6(2), 75-96. DOI 10.54825/cyoc8202. Open access; PDF saved as soderberg.pdf in this folder.
- Structure (read in full text): RQ: how do residents in a Danish public housing neighbourhood targeted by the ghetto legislation engage in resistance? Data: ethnographic fieldwork, August 2021 to January 2023, semi-structured and walking interviews with 18 residents plus participant observation, coded in NVivo. Framework: the "ABC of resistance" combined with place as continuously becoming. Sections: Introduction; Theorising resistance; Contextualisation (ghetto legislation); Research context and methodology; Analysing residents' resistance (avoidance, breaking, constructive); Ambiguous effects of power and resistance; Concluding discussion. The text gives the roadmap in one paragraph. Conclusion: residents re-negotiate the "ghetto" narrative and enact a homeplace.
- Evidence channels: resident interviews, observation, plus a policy-context section on the ghetto list criteria.
- Quote (from the full text): "This article seeks to contribute to the questioning of hegemonic narratives about neighbourhoods designated as 'ghettos' and about processes of urban renewal as being in everyone's best interest by highlighting residents' perspectives and their resistance."
- Relevance (interpretation): a model for the dwelling side of your building-versus-dwelling contrast, though it uses interviews that your essay does not have.

## Theory papers

- Hastings, A. (2004). Stigma and social housing estates: Beyond pathological explanations. Journal of Housing and the Built Environment 19(3), 233-254. DOI 10.1007/s10901-004-0723-y. Existence verified; abstract NOT retrieved (publisher page blocked), so no content claimed.
- Hastings, A. & Dean, J. (2003). Challenging images: tackling stigma through estate regeneration. Policy & Politics 31(2), 171-184. DOI 10.1332/030557303765371663. Quote: "Neighbourhood regeneration initiatives in stigmatised estates have tended not to address the problem of stigma directly, but have largely assumed that an estate's reputation will improve as material conditions on the estate improve." Argument: stigma persists because a problem image creates barriers to the spread of knowledge of change.
- Kearns, A., Kearns, O. & Lawson, L. (2013). Notorious Places: Image, Reputation, Stigma. The Role of Newspapers in Area Reputations for Social Housing Estates. Housing Studies 28(4), 579-598. DOI 10.1080/02673037.2013.759546. Abstract: distinguishes image, reputation and stigma; analyses two estates in printed media over 14 years; "negative and mixed coverage of the estates dominated". Gives a media-evidence design. (Open-access PDF link exists; my download was blocked, so abstract only.)
- Wacquant, L. (2007). Territorial Stigmatization in the Age of Advanced Marginality. Thesis Eleven 91(1), 66-77. DOI 10.1177/0725513607082003. Abstract: three spatial properties of advanced marginality: "territorial fixation and stigmatization, spatial alienation and the dissolution of 'place', and the loss of a hinterland". Note: the open-access PDF behind OpenAlex's link is a Spanish adaptation in Ciencias Sociais Unisinos 43(3), 193-199, not this paper; I did not quote it.

## Ranking: best 3 structural models for your essay

1. Mack (2026). Document-trace across time, three-step argument from welfare ideal to problem to demolition. Closest match to your evidence (planning documents) and to the "deficient/obsolete" framing. Weakness: abstract only, so I cannot confirm section titles.
2. Kallin & Slater (2014). Clear two-part design (how the stigma was made, then how regeneration uses it) ending in a named contradiction. Fits the rent-gap plus reputational gap argument. Abstract only.
3. Arthurson (2001). Three explicit research questions about whose image and what consequence, applied to Australian estates. Suits a comparison of two image-making projects (Waterloo and Green Square). Abstract only; I could not check its sections.

Honourable mention: Soderberg (2024) is the only paper whose full structure I read (explicit roadmap, RQ, framework, data), so it is the safest to imitate for section signposting, even though your essay lacks interviews.
