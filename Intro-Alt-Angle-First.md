# Introduction (alternative: angle first; 1.3–1.4 merged)

Public housing is often discussed as if its quality were a plain fact, yet the way it is understood changes with the plans made for it. Zanardo et al. (2024, 1207) open their study of the Waterloo estate in Sydney with a claim: "The history of public housing in Australia is being rewritten by redevelopment." In a district planned as part of a global city, the imagined future of a place and the planning treatment given to it are closely tied, because a plan needs a past to replace and a future to deliver.

Waterloo, with about 2,000 dwellings on 18 hectares, is among Sydney's largest inner-city public housing estates (Chatterjee et al. 2018), and its renewal has been described as the largest estate renewal proposed in Australia to date (Zanardo et al. 2024, 1208). The plan has changed several times since the state government announced it in 2015. The planning route moved from a state-led process to a council-led one in 2019 (Turner Studio & Turf 2020, PDF p. 6) and back to the state in 2021 (NSW Department of Planning and Environment 2022, PDF p. 11), and in 2023 the target for social and affordable housing across the estate was raised from 34 to 50 per cent (NSW Government 2023).

Its reputation has been contested between the government and its residents. Zanardo et al. (2024, 1208–1209) record officials calling the estate "terrible" (Hazzard) and "not fit for purpose" (Jackson). The housing minister, Rose Jackson, has since said that "old style estates with thousands and thousands of public homes in the one place" are "not functional communities" (Bowring 2026), a remark about that kind of estate and not about Waterloo alone, and the housing activist Karyn Brown answered, "It is very functional" (Bowring 2026). Zanardo et al. (2024, 1236) and Sisson (2024) call this pattern a reputational gap, a term Yrjänä (2026) attributes to Kallin, and Wynne and Rogers (2021) show how residents experience the threat of displacement before any demolition. This paper takes the reputational gap as its background and asks a different question of the plans.

This paper asks whether the renewal of Waterloo breaks that circle or relocates it, and argues that the answer is decided where the planning documents fix floor space but leave return to statements. Sydney's council, arguing for more affordable housing at Green Square Town Centre, describes the market in which this renewal takes place: "Gentrification of inner city neighbourhoods has exacerbated relative inequality among those who can and cannot afford housing," and there is "a growing disconnect between affluent households able to afford private market housing and highly socio-economically disadvantaged households living in inner city public housing estates" (City of Sydney n.d.). The official case for renewing such estates answers a version of the same divide. Future Directions describes disadvantage that clusters in estates and is "passed on through multiple generations" (NSW Government 2016, 24), and mixed tenure is its remedy. It reads the plan's tenure mix, the destinations of relocated tenants and the promise of return against the five steps of the vicious circle of segregation (Rosengren 2026; Tammaru et al. 2021), and asks which of these the planning instrument secures and which it leaves to announcements, in a city where the median price of attached dwellings more than doubled between 2006 and 2026 (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2026). The sections follow the four units of the course: the global city, housing, segregation and gentrification.
