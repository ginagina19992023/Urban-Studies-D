# Introduction

Mixing tenures is a standard remedy for the concentration of disadvantage in public housing estates. The NSW Government justified renewing its estates with a diagnosis, that disadvantage clusters in estates and is “passed on through multiple generations” (NSW Government 2016, PDF p. 24), and with a remedy, a 70:30 split between private and social dwellings “to enable more integrated communities” (NSW Government 2016, PDF p. 9). This paper asks whether the renewal of the Waterloo estate in Sydney breaks that circle of segregation or only moves it, and argues that the answer depends on what the planning rules actually guarantee: they fix how much floor space goes to social housing, but the right of return appears only as a statement of intent.

Waterloo, with about 2,000 dwellings on 18 hectares, is among Sydney's largest inner-city public housing estates (Chatterjee et al. 2018), and its renewal has been described as the largest estate renewal proposed in Australia to date (Zanardo et al. 2024, 1208). The plan has changed several times since the state government announced it in 2015. The planning route, which decides what the rules can fix, moved from a state-led process to a council-led one in 2019 (Turner Studio & Turf 2020, PDF p. 6) and back to the state in 2021 (NSW Department of Planning and Environment 2022, PDF p. 11), and in 2023 the target for social and affordable housing across the estate was raised from 34 to 50 per cent (NSW Government 2023).

The renewal takes place in an expensive and unequal market. Sydney’s council, arguing for more affordable housing at Green Square Town Centre, describes it: “Gentrification of inner city neighbourhoods has exacerbated relative inequality among those who can and cannot afford housing,” and there is “a growing disconnect between affluent households able to afford private market housing and highly socio-economically disadvantaged households living in inner city public housing estates” (City of Sydney n.d.). In that market the median price of attached dwellings more than doubled between 2006 and 2026 (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2026), and the sale of private dwellings funds the renewal (Zanardo et al. 2024, 1208).

Existing work on Waterloo concerns reputation and displacement. Zanardo et al. (2024, 1236) and Sisson (2024) describe a reputational gap, a term Yrjänä (2026) attributes to Kallin, in which the present of a place is devalued and its imagined potential is set against it, and Wynne and Rogers (2021) show how residents experience the threat of displacement before any demolition. Officials and residents describe the estate differently: Hazzard called it “terrible” and Jackson “not fit for purpose” (Zanardo et al. 2024, 1208–1209), while the housing activist Karyn Brown answered, “It is very functional” (Bowring 2026). This paper takes reputation as background and reads the plan against the five steps of the vicious circle of segregation.

The paper is a document analysis. Its materials are the planning documents for Waterloo South (the plan finalisation report, the final planning proposal and the Design Guide, all from 2022), government statements and media releases from 2015 to 2026, news reports, and published studies of Waterloo and of other Sydney estates. I sorted each protection for tenants by where it appears: in a binding planning control, in the Design Guide, in a statement inside the planning record, or only in an announcement.

This paper reads the plan’s tenure mix, the destinations of relocated tenants and the promise of return against the five steps of the vicious circle of segregation (Rosengren 2026; Tammaru et al. 2021), and asks which of them the planning rules guarantee and which are only promised in announcements. The sections take four bodies of urban theory in turn: the global city, housing, segregation and gentrification.
