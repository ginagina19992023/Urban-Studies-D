## Working Title

**"Who Captures the Uplift? Public Land, Territorial Stigma and the Demolition of Sydney's Waterloo Estate"**

(中文工作标题:《谁捕获了增值？公有土地、地域污名与悉尼滑铁卢公屋区的拆除》)

> **为什么不是"悉尼房价上涨"**：那个题目按课程自身标准属于"太宽"，7 页写不下，且缺少可指认的政策抓手。Waterloo 保留了"房价/地价上涨"这个核心关切——**增值（uplift）**正是房价问题的空间形态——但把它锚定在一块 12.3 公顷的具体土地、一批具体的人、一条具体的政策链上。

## The phenomenon (作业任务 ①)

The demolition of the Waterloo public housing estate in inner-south Sydney — the largest and most concentrated public housing estate in Australia — which moved from two decades of announced-but-deferred "renewal" into actual demolition in mid-2026.

The estate as it stood contained **750 social housing dwellings and 120 private dwellings** across a 12.3-hectare precinct bounded by Cope, Raglan, George, Wellington, Gibson, Kellick, Pitt and McEvoy Streets, directly adjoining Waterloo Metro Station and within walking distance of Redfern and Green Square. It is to be replaced, over 10–15 years, by roughly **3,300 dwellings** built by a consortium led by the private developer **Stockland** in partnership with **Homes NSW**, at heights of 2 to 33 storeys.

What makes this a *phenomenon* rather than merely a project is the collision that occurred in mid-2026: demolition of the first tranche (~150 dwellings) proceeded while residents and supporters occupied the site from 24 May 2026, an occupation that lasted more than three weeks before being cleared by police.

---

## Core research question

**When a state demolishes public housing on publicly owned land beside a publicly funded metro station, who captures the value that this sequence of public investments creates — and what makes that capture politically sayable?**

## Thesis statement

Waterloo is best understood not as a housing-supply project that happens to be contested, but as a **value-capture arrangement made politically possible by territorial stigma**. Three public actions — public ownership of the land, public investment in Waterloo Metro, and public authority to rezone — together produced a very large uplift in the site's potential ground rent. The state then structured the redevelopment so that up to **50% of the resulting floorspace is market housing delivered by a private developer**. The essay's argument is that this transfer was made sayable, and its alternatives unsayable, by a sustained official denigration of the estate's physical and social condition — a denigration that Zanardo, Sisson, Logan and McLaughlan (2024) show, through direct architectural assessment against the government's own apartment design standards, was not conducted in good faith.

The secondary argument concerns **the timing of displacement**. Wynne and Rogers (2021), on ethnographic fieldwork conducted at Waterloo between 2010 and 2017, showed that displacement had already occurred years before anyone moved: the permanent threat of redevelopment restructured residents' spatial, social and temporal relationship to the place while they remained physically in situ ("emplaced displacement"). Writing in 2026 offers something their fieldwork could not: **the opportunity to observe what happens when a decade-long anticipatory displacement finally converts into the physical event it anticipated** — and to ask whether the physical event, when it arrives, is experienced as rupture or as confirmation.

| Marcuse, exclusionary displacement | Applies to the 10–15 year gap between demolition and completion, and to who is eligible to return |

### The Helsinki–Sydney bridge (原创连线)

The definitive scholarly account of stigma at Waterloo is co-authored by **Alistair Sisson** — the same scholar whose work on quantified territorial stigma (*Denigrating by numbers*, IJURR 2021; and *Territory and territorial stigmatisation*, Progress in Human Geography 2021) anchors the stigma argument in Yrjänä's (2026) Helsinki study read earlier in this course. This is a genuine, citable continuity rather than a rhetorical parallel: **one theoretical apparatus, applied by its own author to both cases.**

That makes an unusually clean comparison available. Helsinki and Sydney share the two decisive structural conditions — substantial public land ownership and planning authority — and both invoke social mix against concentrated disadvantage. Yrjänä's finding is that Helsinki uses these powers to *de-risk private investment* rather than to capture value publicly ("privatizing profits and socializing losses"). Waterloo is the same choice made under a much larger uplift and with far less deferral: **public land, public metro, public rezoning, private capture of half the floorspace.**

## Policy implications, and whether policy strengthens or weakens the phenomenon (作业任务 ③④ — 硬性要求)

This must be argued in both directions, not asserted in one. The honest position is that NSW policy currently does both.

| Policy / instrument | Strengthens the phenomenon | Weakens it |
|---|---|---|
| State-led rezoning of the precinct | Converts uplift into developable floorspace at state discretion | — |
| 70:30 social-mix orthodoxy in NSW estate renewal | Sets the default that most of a renewed estate becomes market housing | — |
| Private development partnership (Stockland) | Positions a private party to capture the market-housing share | — |
| Metro-adjacent location / TOD logic | Maximises the uplift, raising the incentive to redevelop rather than repair | — |
| **2025–26 increase of social + affordable to 50%** (from ~34%), up to ~500 additional homes | — | A real, material concession — the single strongest counter-evidence to a pure critique |
| First Nations allocation (20% of social, 15% of affordable) | — | Targeted redress with an enforceable quota |
| City of Sydney counter-proposal, developed with resident participation | — | Demonstrates that an alternative plan was technically available |
| Shelter NSW alternative economic modelling | — | Contests the claim that demolition was financially necessary |

**The argument to make**: the concessions are real and should be credited, but they are concessions *within* a frame that was already settled — the frame in which demolition, rather than repair, is the baseline. Zanardo et al.'s contribution is to show that this baseline was established by avoiding the assessment that might have unsettled it. Policy is therefore weakening the phenomenon *at the margin of tenure ratios* while strengthening it *at the level of the frame*.

## Counter-evidence to engage seriously (rubric: 5 分要求"考虑并反驳对立证据")

A high-grade essay must not treat the government's case as empty. It is not:

1. **Absolute social housing supply rises.** Roughly 1,000 social + 600 affordable dwellings replace 750 social. In absolute terms this is more social housing, not less.
2. **The estate is genuinely ageing**, and no serious account denies that significant capital works were needed.
3. **Inner-city land is scarce**; densifying a metro-adjacent 12.3 ha site is defensible planning on its own terms.

The essay's response should be structural rather than dismissive:
- **Time**: a 10–15 year delivery horizon means the supply gain is deferred while the loss is immediate. Use the Housing Studies (2024) work on system-wide supply impacts of estate renewal to make this measurable rather than rhetorical.
- **Composition**: net dwelling counts do not track *who* occupies them, or whether the dispersed community reconstitutes.
- **Necessity**: "ageing" establishes that something must be done; it does not establish that demolition was the required response — which is exactly the gap Zanardo et al. document, and exactly what Shelter NSW's costings contest.

## Draft structure (7 pages ≈ 3,500–4,000 words)

1. **Introduction** — hook: in June 2026 police cleared an occupation so that demolition of Australia's largest public housing estate could begin. Question: who captures the uplift, and what made the transfer sayable? Preview structure.
2. **From rent gap to publicly manufactured uplift** — Smith; the Waterloo gap as a product of public metro investment and public rezoning rather than private disinvestment.
3. **Stigma as the enabling condition** — Wacquant, Kallin, Sisson; Zanardo et al.'s architectural assessment as the evidentiary core; the estate denigrated so demolition became the baseline.
4. **Displacement before and after the event** — Davidson → Wynne & Rogers's emplaced displacement (2010–2017); the 2026 conversion of anticipation into event.
5. **Policy: strengthening and weakening** — the table above, argued in both directions; the 50% uplift credited honestly; counter-evidence engaged.
6. **Discussion: Helsinki and Sydney** — same powers, same social-mix discourse, same choice to route value to private capital; Sydney as the sharper case because the uplift is larger and the deferral shorter.
7. **Conclusion** — reframe: the interesting variable in state-led renewal is not whether social mix is invoked (it always is) but **who the ownership structure positions to capture the uplift**. Public ownership does not by itself secure public capture — Waterloo and Helsinki both show public landowners choosing to route value outward. The question this raises for planning is what accountability mechanism, if any, could make public land ownership actually function as a value-capture instrument.
